How Moonat works

Pick one or more places on the Moon and a time (or a whole mission). Moonat puts the Sun and Earth exactly where they are in that sky and checks whether the local hills hide them.

What the numbers mean

Elevation
How high a body is above the flat, ideal horizon, in degrees. 0° is the horizon line, 90° is straight up. A positive value does not guarantee a view: a hill or crater rim can still block it.
Azimuth
The compass direction, in degrees clockwise from lunar north: 0° north, 90° east, 180° south, 270° west.
Visible disk
How much of the Sun's or Earth's disk clears the real terrain horizon. 100% means fully in view, 0% means hidden.
Sunlight (W/m²)
Solar power arriving on a panel that faces the Sun, or on flat ground. About 1361 W/m² at Earth's average distance from the Sun, scaled by the actual distance and by how much of the Sun is visible.
Earth phase
How much of Earth's disk is lit. It is the opposite of the Moon's phase seen from Earth: at new Moon, Earth looks full.
Sets in / rises in
Time until the body next disappears behind (or clears) the horizon. For Earth this is the end of the direct radio window.
Height and direction
The words the sky view uses for elevation and azimuth. "Hidden behind terrain" means the body is above the flat horizon but a hill or crater rim is higher in that direction; "below the horizon" means it is below the flat horizon itself.
Time since start (T+)
How far into each scenario the sky is shown. Several missions are compared at the same time since their own start.
Close-up (6× zoom)
The round inset magnifies the Earth (and the Sun while hills or the Earth cover part of it), so you can see its phase and an eclipse. "6× zoom" means it looks six times larger than in the main view.
Exposure
Eye: the stars stay visible in daylight, dimmed, to help you find your way. Camera: set for the sunlit ground, so the stars vanish while the Sun is up, as in the Apollo photographs.
LOLA
The Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter on NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter. It measured the height of the Moon's surface with a laser; Moonat builds the hills and the horizon from it.
Libration
A slow rocking of the Moon as seen from Earth, by up to about 8°, because its orbit is an ellipse and is tilted. Seen from the Moon, it makes Earth drift in a small loop in the sky over a month, so near the poles and the edge of the near side Earth can set and rise.

Why the far side never sees Earth

The Moon always shows the same face to Earth, because it spins exactly once per orbit. Seen from the near side, Earth hangs in almost the same spot in the sky, rocking by a few degrees (libration). From the far side, Earth never rises at all. That is why China's Chang'e 4 lander needed the Queqiao relay satellite to talk home.

Why terrain matters

Near the poles the Sun skims the horizon, so a single ridge can decide whether a lander gets power. Moonat uses NASA LOLA laser altimetry to trace the real horizon around catalogued sites, out to more than 100 km, including the curvature of the Moon. For a point you pick yourself, Moonat fetches the same kind of LOLA elevation for that spot from NASA Moon Trek (20 m near the south pole, 30 m near the poles, 118 m elsewhere) and builds the horizon and the 3D ground in your browser within a few seconds. Until it arrives, or if Moon Trek cannot be reached, the horizon comes from a coarser grid shipped with the site (about 3.8 km per cell), labelled "approximate".

Data and accuracy

  • Positions: JPL DE421 planetary ephemeris and the precise lunar orientation (libration included), with light-time and aberration. The Sun and Earth directions agree with JPL Horizons to better than one arcsecond ().
  • Terrain: NASA Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter laser altimeter (LOLA) elevation models, from the NASA Planetary Data System.
  • Map: NASA Moon Trek, LRO Wide Angle Camera global mosaic. Near the poles the globe shows shaded relief made from LOLA elevation models (240 m per pixel around the south pole). Its Layers button adds NASA's maps of permanently shadowed ground and of the most sunlit ground (LOLA, from the NASA PGDA, Mazarico et al. 2011).
  • Earth: NASA Blue Marble and City Lights images, turned with the real rotation of the Earth.
  • Stars: Yale Bright Star Catalogue (stars to magnitude 6); constellation lines from d3-celestial.
  • Missions: landing coordinates and times from NASA NSSDCA, the Apollo Lunar Surface Journal and LROC. The potential Artemis sites are the candidate landing regions NASA announced in October 2024, placed at approximate points, since exact landing sites are not chosen yet.
  • Flags: the flag of the nation that flew each mission, unaltered, from flag-icons (MIT licence) and, for the Soviet Union, Wikimedia Commons (public domain).
  • Frames: selenographic latitude and east-positive longitude (Mean Earth / polar axis frame). Times are UTC. The eye is 2 m above the ground. The Moon has no air, so there is no refraction.
